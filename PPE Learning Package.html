Welcome to the Learning Package

PPE is an important barrier during routine care, but it doesn’t work alone. Safety depends on risk assessment, correct selection, proper use, safe removal, appropriate disposal and hand hygiene. This package builds those skills through quick activities, real cases and feedback.

🎯 Overall aim

To enhance nurses’ knowledge and clinical decision-making skills in the appropriate selection, safe use and removal of personal protective equipment (PPE) to minimize cross-contamination and reduce the risk of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs).

Target audience: registered nurses. Teaching approach: online self-directed learning through interactive presentations, demonstrations, case scenarios, quizzes and interactive practice activities.

Estimated completion time: 45–60 minutes, including the pre-test, three modules, interactive activities, module assessments, post-test and reflection. Supervised hands-on competency assessment is separate.

    Why this package uses adult learning principles

    This learning package is designed for registered nurses who bring previous clinical experience, prefer relevant learning, and need opportunities to apply new knowledge immediately in practice.

    1. Need to know

    Each module begins with clear objectives and explains how PPE decisions prevent cross-contamination and protect patients and staff.

    2. Self-concept

    Use the “Go to this objective” buttons to select what to study, revisit demonstrations and repeat practice activities at your own pace.

    3. Prior experience

    Clinical cases, safe-versus-unsafe examples and reflective prompts invite learners to compare the teaching with their own ward practice.

    4. Readiness to learn

    Lessons focus on tasks nurses encounter in daily practice: selecting PPE, donning, doffing, disposal and responding to unsafe behaviours.

    5. Problem-centred orientation

    Choose Your Shield!, Spot the Safety Mistake!, Be the PPE Auditor! and Fix the Risk! require decisions about authentic clinical problems.

    6. Intrinsic motivation

    Immediate explanations, stars, activity badges, progress tracking and celebratory animations encourage continued learning and mastery.

    What are HAIs?

    Infections patients acquire while receiving healthcare that were not present or incubating when care began. They can mean increased illness, longer stays, added costs, more antimicrobial use and preventable harm. Nurses have frequent, close patient contact, so you play a central role in prevention.

    Guidance and supporting evidence: (CDC, 2024)

    Standard precautionsTransmission-based precautions

    Standard precautions in brief

    Apply to all patients, regardless of known or suspected infection: hand hygiene, risk-based PPE, respiratory hygiene, sharps safety, safe injection practices, environmental cleaning, and careful handling of equipment, linen and waste. PPE choice rests on anticipated exposure.

    Transmission-based precautions in brief

    Added to standard precautions when an infection needs more. Tap a type.

    Exact PPE follows the infection, procedure, exposure and facility policy.

    🧠 Clinical scenario 1

    Helping a patient with personal hygiene; no known infection. Do you automatically need gloves, gown, mask and goggles?

    No. Select PPE for the anticipated exposure. Standard precautions apply to every patient, but not every PPE item for every interaction.

    Objective 1

    Selecting PPE by clinical risk

    Guidance and supporting evidence: (CDC, 2024)

    🧤 Gloves

    Barrier for exposure to blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, non-intact skin or contaminated material. They do not replace hand hygiene: clean hands before and after. Change between patients and when moving from a contaminated to a cleaner body site. Don’t touch phones, pens, keyboards or door handles in contaminated gloves. (CDC, 2024)

    Mask vs respirator

    A respirator is fitted to seal against the face; follow fit-testing and seal-check policy.

    🧠 Spot the error

    A nurse removes contaminated gloves and immediately takes out her phone before hand hygiene.

    Contaminated hands can transfer microorganisms to the phone. Perform hand hygiene at the right point after glove removal.

    🧪 Clinical challenge

    While removing PPE, a nurse touches the front of her mask with bare hands. Main concern?

    Self-contamination. The front may be contaminated; remove by straps, ties or designated areas.
    Objective 2

    Auditing PPE practice against guidelines

    Guidance and supporting evidence: (CDC, 2024)

    📋 The PPE compliance checklist

    Audit practice against these seven recommended infection-prevention points. Follow your facility's policy as the final standard.

    1. Hand hygiene before donning
    2. Right PPE for the task and precautions
    3. PPE intact and worn correctly
    4. No touching of face or clean surfaces with contaminated PPE
    5. Safe doffing technique
    6. Hand hygiene after doffing
    7. Correct disposal of used PPE

    My reflection on designing this learning package

    Personal self-reflection using Gibbs’ Reflective Cycle (1988)

    Description

    As part of my Master's in Nursing programme, I developed an interactive learning package on the safe use of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) for registered nurses. The process involved planning the learning content, developing objectives using Bloom's Taxonomy, incorporating adult learning principles, and designing interactive activities and assessments. I also created a website to deliver the learning package.

    Feelings

    Initially, I felt both excited and nervous when I learned that we had to develop a website. I considered it a challenging task and was concerned about whether I could complete it within the given timeframe. I was also unsure how to organize the content and activities effectively. However, once I started working on the website, I gradually became more comfortable and confident.

    Evaluation

    One of the most enjoyable aspects was exploring different website themes, interactive features, and quiz designs. These activities made the development process interesting and encouraged my creativity. However, creating the website and ensuring that assessments aligned with the learning objectives were challenging. I also realized that planning and time management could have been better.

    Analysis

    This experience helped me understand that developing an effective learning package involves more than presenting information. Learning objectives, teaching activities, and assessments must be carefully aligned to support meaningful learning. Applying Knowles' Adult Learning Theory helped me recognize the importance of creating relevant, engaging, and problem-centred activities for registered nurses. I also developed a better understanding of how Bloom's Taxonomy can guide the formulation of measurable learning objectives and how technology can support nursing education.

    Conclusion

    Overall, developing this learning package was a valuable learning experience that strengthened my knowledge of educational planning and instructional design. Although I encountered challenges, I gained confidence in using technology to create interactive educational materials. Looking back, I could have approached the task more effectively by planning earlier, organizing the content more systematically, and testing the activities throughout the development process.

    Action Plan

    In future educational projects, I will develop a clear plan and timeline before beginning, establish measurable learning objectives, and ensure that assessments are aligned with those objectives from the start. I will also test interactive activities earlier and seek feedback to identify areas for improvement. Most importantly, I intend to apply the knowledge and skills gained from this experience when planning and delivering educational sessions for nurses in clinical practice.
